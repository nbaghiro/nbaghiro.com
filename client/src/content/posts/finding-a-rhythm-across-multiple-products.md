I’ve noticed something about the way I like to work: having several products in progress makes building more enjoyable for me.

That feels slightly awkward to admit. I understand the case for focusing on one product. There are fewer decisions competing for attention, a clearer audience to learn from, and one place to put the next hour of work. Those are real advantages.

But I like the variety. I enjoy moving between different problems, seeing an idea take shape, and finding that something I learned in one product helps with another. It keeps me interested in the work itself.

We now have several mobile apps ready to submit to the stores. Each is built around a problem we think is worth solving. That has me thinking about a small mobile app studio as a way to organise the work.

I don’t know yet whether that will become a sustainable business. I do know that I want to explore it without losing the part of building that makes me want to come back tomorrow.

## A different problem can be a useful change of pace

There are days when I want to work through a difficult technical problem. There are other days when I get more satisfaction from improving a screen, simplifying an interaction, or making a short demo that finally explains what a product does.

Having a few products gives me room for those different kinds of work. After a focused stretch on one app, spending time on another can help me return with a clearer view of the first.

That is different from constantly interrupting myself. I still need enough time inside a problem to understand it. Switching every time something gets difficult would leave me with a collection of unfinished decisions.

The rhythm I want is more deliberate: choose a useful piece of work, stay with it, and leave a clear note about what comes next. Then a change of project can feel like a fresh start without requiring me to reconstruct everything when I return.

## The learning travels between products

One of the enjoyable parts is noticing how often a lesson applies somewhere else.

A confusing first screen raises questions about onboarding. An awkward loading state makes me think about what happens on a poor connection. A mobile layout forces me to decide what information someone actually needs in that moment.

Those questions come up across products, even when the people using them have very different goals.

Some of that learning can become shared code. Some belongs in a checklist or a design habit. And some needs to stay specific to the app. A pattern that works for someone managing appointments may be a poor fit for someone opening an app for a quick personal task.

I want to get better at recognising which kind of reuse is useful. Copying a component is easy. Understanding why it worked takes more care.

The same applies to infrastructure. A reliable release process or a good way to diagnose a crash could help every app. Trying to force every product into the same navigation or data model could make each one harder to use.

## What I mean by a small app studio

The studio idea is fairly simple at this stage: a small collection of focused apps, with a shared foundation for the work that repeats.

Each app should have a clear answer to three questions: who is it for, what does it help them do, and why would they return?

Behind those individual products, I’d like a consistent approach to testing, releases, support and maintenance. That would give me a place to improve the process once and carry the useful parts into the next app.

![Three distinct apps and audiences sit above a shared studio foundation for testing, releases and support. This is a proposed structure.](figure:studio-foundation)

That shared foundation would also need boundaries. A change to a common component should be checked in the apps that use it. Each app still needs its own release decision. Sharing code doesn’t remove the responsibility to understand what a change does to someone’s experience.

I also want the products to stay small enough to explain clearly. It is easy to keep adding features because the code is already there or because an idea worked elsewhere. The more useful question is whether that addition helps the person this particular app is for.

A studio structure appeals to me because it gives the collection a practical shape. It gives me somewhere to put the repeated work while leaving room for the products to be different.

## Ready to submit is the start of another kind of work

Getting an app ready for the store is satisfying. It means a lot of small decisions have finally come together into something someone can use.

It also leaves plenty to learn. I want to see whether people understand the purpose from the store page, whether they reach a useful moment inside the app, and whether they have a reason to come back.

A technically working app can still be confusing. A feature I enjoyed building may matter less than a small task someone needs to finish every day. Those are things I expect to understand much better once people use the products outside my own development routine.

For each app, I want a short set of questions to guide that learning:

- Can someone explain what the app helps them do?
- Where do they get stuck before completing the main task?
- What brings them back, if anything?
- What support or maintenance does the app require?

Those answers should influence where I spend time next. If one product starts earning much more attention from users, I want to be willing to give it more of mine.

## Marketing needs a place in the routine

I’ve been spending more time learning marketing, and working on several products makes its demands clearer.

Each app needs its own explanation. The audience, the useful example, and the reason to care can all change. Having plenty of screens to show doesn’t automatically give me a message that makes sense to someone seeing the product for the first time.

I do have a lot of material to experiment with: real screens, workflows and demos. I want to build a repeatable habit around turning that material into something useful, then learning from the response.

That thinking is also behind Dailies, an early idea for organising product briefs, creative variations and the results of marketing experiments. It is still an idea I’m exploring. The immediate need is simple: keep track of what I’m trying to communicate, what I’ve made, and what I’ve learned.

The studio would need time for that work alongside development. Otherwise, I could keep making apps more polished without helping the right people discover them.

## Giving the work a rhythm

The part I’m still working out is how to preserve variety while making commitments I can keep.

I don’t expect every app to receive equal attention every week. A more useful structure might be one primary focus for a stretch of time, a clear maintenance list for the other apps, and some room for exploration.

![Three kinds of work: one primary focus, maintenance for existing apps, and space to explore. The balance changes with user needs.](figure:studio-attention)

The primary focus would have a concrete next outcome, such as preparing a release or improving a difficult first-use experience. Maintenance would cover the obligations that continue even when an app is not the main project. Exploration would give new ideas somewhere to go without making every idea an immediate commitment.

I’d also need a reasonable way to pause work. Writing down the current state, the next decision, and any outstanding user commitments should make returning easier. It should also make it clearer when a project needs to stay paused.

There will be tradeoffs. More apps mean more things that can break, more messages to answer, and more releases to keep up with. Enjoying the variety doesn’t make those costs disappear.

## Leaving room to find out

I’m comfortable saying that this is an experiment in how I want to work.

I enjoy building several products. I like the change of pace and the way ideas move between them. A small mobile app studio seems like a structure worth trying as these apps reach the stores.

I’m less certain about how many products I can support well, which ones people will find useful, or how the balance will change once they are in use. Those questions need experience, and I expect the answers to change my plans.

For now, I want to give the apps a chance to meet their users, pay attention to what happens, and keep making room for the work I enjoy. I’m curious to see what that turns into.
